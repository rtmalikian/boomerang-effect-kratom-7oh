# Conference abstract

**Title:** When a Warning Advertises: Boomerang Effects in Anti-Drug Campaigns and the Public Communication of 7-Hydroxymitragynine Control

**Author:** Raphael Malikian, MBBS, BSc (Hons). Independent researcher, Palmdale, CA, USA. The author trained in family medicine and is in recovery from substance use. He is not currently licensed to practise medicine; this submission is scholarly commentary and contains no clinical or medication recommendations.

**Background.** Anti-drug campaigns and prohibition announcements assume that naming a danger reduces it. Health communication research on the boomerang effect shows that assumption failing under identifiable conditions, and federal action against concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products in 2025 has raised the question again for a product most of the public had never heard of.

**Objective.** To summarise the evidence on iatrogenic effects of drug prevention messaging, apply it to the public communication of 7-OH control, and set out design principles for campaigns and care that warn without advertising.

**Evidence reviewed.** Psychological reactance, the motive to restore a threatened freedom, is supported by meta-analysis (Rains, 2013: 20 studies, 4,942 participants). The evaluation of the US National Youth Anti-Drug Media Campaign found no favourable effect and an association between heavier exposure and weaker intentions to avoid marijuana (Hornik et al., Am J Public Health 2008); a Cochrane review found pooled mass-media effects on drug use were null, with iatrogenic effects in individual campaigns (Ferri et al., 2013). Meta-analyses of DARE found effect sizes on drug use of 0.06 (Ennett et al., 1994) and d = 0.023, nonsignificant (West and O'Neal, 2004), with no advantage at 10-year follow-up (Lynam et al., 1999). Tobacco industry parent-targeted advertisements were associated with stronger intentions to smoke in older students (odds ratio 1.12; Wakefield et al., 2006), while the autonomy-respecting "truth" campaign accounted for about 22 percent of the 1999 to 2002 decline in US youth smoking (Farrelly et al., 2005). For the United Kingdom mephedrone episode, the EMCDDA recorded users reporting first awareness through mass-media coverage, and search analysis showed "mephedrone buy" queries spiking within days of death coverage that later proved to be false alarms (Forsyth, 2012). No direct evidence yet links 7-OH publicity to increased use; the claim made here is a risk claim, stated as such. On framing, calling addiction a "chronically relapsing brain disease" reduced blame but also reduced prognostic optimism in a national randomised study (Kelly et al., Addiction 2021), which supports disease framing without a permanent addict identity.

**Conclusions.** Prevention communication should be pretested for reactance, audited for product detail, and evaluated against behavioural outcomes with monitoring for iatrogenic effects. Care should be individualised, time-bound by goals the patient chooses, and framed as treatment of a chronic disease that a person has, rather than an identity a person is.
